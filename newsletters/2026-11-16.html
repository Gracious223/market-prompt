Public Markets 101
Essential concepts for your role

Six more building blocks of market literacy — covering valuation, risk, and how capital is measured and priced. These are the terms your portfolio managers and analysts use daily, and the ones that come up most when evaluating whether AI-generated outputs are trustworthy enough to act on.

Basis Points (bps)
One hundredth of one percentage point. 25 bps = 0.25%. Used to express changes in interest rates, credit spreads, and fee structures. If a PM says "spreads widened 50 bps," they mean 0.5 percentage points.
Beta
A measure of how sensitive a stock or portfolio is to market movements. Beta of 1 moves with the market; above 1 is more volatile; below 1 is more defensive. High-beta portfolios amplify both gains and losses.
Short Selling
Borrowing a security and selling it, hoping to buy it back cheaper later. Short sellers profit when prices fall. AI tools that surface negative catalysts or anomalies are increasingly used to generate short ideas.
Market Maker
A firm that quotes both buy and sell prices for a security, providing liquidity. Market makers profit from the bid-ask spread. AI is now used heavily in market-making to price risk and manage inventory in milliseconds.
Earnings Per Share (EPS)
Net profit divided by total shares outstanding. EPS is the most watched per-share profitability metric. Analysts use AI to model and forecast EPS across earnings seasons — accuracy against consensus is a key evaluation criterion.
Net Asset Value (NAV)
The value of a fund's total assets minus its liabilities, divided by shares outstanding. NAV is the price at which you buy or sell a mutual fund or ETF. AI tools that model portfolio NAV in real time are a high-value buy-side use case.

Research · arXiv

FinRobot: Multi-Agent Framework Splits Equity Research into Data, Reasoning, and Thesis Agents

FinRobot (arXiv: 2411.08804) proposes a three-agent architecture for equity research: one agent for data aggregation, one for analyst-style reasoning, one for investment thesis writing. Each agent specialises and hands off, reducing hallucination risk versus a single LLM producing the whole output.

Why It Matters

Specialised, chained agents are more auditable than monolithic outputs. This architecture maps directly to how compliance teams will want to review AI-generated research — step by step, not as a black box.

Research & Think About
  • Read FinRobot: arXiv 2411.08804 — focus on how the agents hand off and how errors are contained between steps
  • What are the regulatory implications of a multi-agent chain producing a research recommendation? Who is accountable at each step?
  • Understand what "hallucination containment" means in a multi-step AI pipeline — ask your data scientists how they test for it

BoE/FCA Survey 2026: 86% of UK Financial Firms Use AI — But Only 2% Let Agents Act Independently

The fourth joint Bank of England / FCA survey, covering 196 firms, finds 86% using AI (up from 75%) and 80% with deployed AI agents. Yet only 2% give agents broad autonomy. Cloud concentration with a small number of third-party providers is flagged as a systemic risk alongside model accuracy and consumer outcomes.

Why It Matters

The 80%-deploy / 2%-trust gap is your competitive landscape. The firms that solve human-in-the-loop design — and can demonstrate it to regulators — will unlock the other 78% of agent deployments.
